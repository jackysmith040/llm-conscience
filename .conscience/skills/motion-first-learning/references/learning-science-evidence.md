# Learning-science evidence

Use this reference to ground design choices without overstating neuroscience.

## Design principles

Treat learning as an interaction among attention, working memory, prior knowledge, schema formation, meaningful encoding, retrieval and reconstruction, consolidation over time, feedback, transfer, metacognition, motivation, and practice. Judge learning by later performance, not by immediate fluency or familiarity.

Use motion when it makes a transition, causal relation, spatial change, or mechanism easier to represent. Control extraneous movement, split attention, redundant text, and simultaneous changes. Freeze irrelevant elements and reveal complex systems progressively.

After explanation, use retrieval, prediction, self-explanation, application, variation, delayed review, interleaving, and independent production. Rereading and passive viewing may support orientation but are insufficient evidence of durable mastery.

Use neuroscience cautiously. Do not claim that a particular activity rewires a specific pathway, diagnose a condition, or infer a fixed learning style. Prefer cognitive and behavioral claims that can be tested in the session.

## Evidence classes

Label claims as one of:

- **Evidence:** directly supported by a cited high-quality source.
- **Design inference:** a reasonable instructional implication from evidence.
- **Working hypothesis:** a candidate adaptation to test with this learner.
- **Source-derived:** stated or shown in the supplied material.
- **Unverified:** plausible but requiring checking.

## Operational rule

When a learning intervention is proposed, state its predicted benefit and specify the evidence that would count as success: reconstruction, delayed retrieval, fewer repeated errors, transfer, accurate explanation, or more independent work.
